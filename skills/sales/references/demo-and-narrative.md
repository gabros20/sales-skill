# Demo and narrative

Purpose: what a product demo can honestly claim to do, and why the demo is the answer to what the
discovery call surfaced rather than a fixed script or feature tour. No benchmark figure in this
territory survives this pack's figure-survival rule — that absence is stated plainly here, not
papered over with a stat that doesn't hold up.

Read when: building or reviewing a demo flow, deciding what to show and in what order, writing the
narrative arc for a sales call that includes a product walkthrough; deciding whether to build a
demo or mockup *before* any conversation and send it as the first touch.

Skip when: the rapport-vs-methodology evidence question for the conversation before the demo (→
`discovery-calls.md`); what to say when the prospect objects during or after the demo (→
`objections-and-enablement.md`); how a proposal or quote follows the demo (→
`proposals-pricing-and-negotiation.md`); whether "Demo" as a CRM stage name means anything (→
`crm-and-pipeline.md`).

Inputs: whatever the discovery call actually surfaced — the stated business need, the stakeholders
in the room, the evaluation criteria named so far.

Produces: a demo structure keyed to what was discovered, not a rehearsed default; an honest
statement of what evidence exists for demo methodology (little to none) so the reader isn't left
hunting for a citation that doesn't exist.

## 1. The honest evidence position

No source reached anywhere in this research — vendor, practitioner, or academic — publishes a
measured win-rate or close-rate lift attributable to any demo methodology, structure, or script.
The discipline's own most-cited reference point is a book (Peter Cohan's *Great Demo!*), named
alongside SPIN Selling and The Challenger Sale in a curated sales-engineering bibliography
([vladar107/awesome-sales-engineer](https://github.com/vladar107/awesome-sales-engineer/blob/fdb7823eae1084f60e10bd8d83795677e29afec1/README.md),
CC0) — a reading-list pointer, not a study. No channel in this build read the book's own evidence
base directly, so its claims are **unverified, not debunked** — the same posture this pack takes
toward every named methodology it hasn't independently checked (`icp-and-qualification.md`,
"MEDDIC/MEDDPICC: evidence and legal status"). A book existing, or being widely recommended, is not
the same as its claims having been tested.

**No demo-outcome benchmark needs a recognise-and-correct treatment, because none traceable
circulates.** That is itself the honest position for this file, not a gap this pack forgot to fill
(the demo-first genre's cost figures, §6, are a different claim — about the seller's cost).

## 2. Demo as answer, not tour

The default failure mode this file argues against is the demo as a fixed feature tour, walked
identically regardless of who's in the room. The alternative this file teaches: a demo is the
answer to specific questions a discovery call already raised. If nothing was surfaced worth
answering, there is nothing yet worth demoing — running the tour anyway substitutes activity for
evidence of fit, the same substitution this pack's forecasting and methodology sections warn
against elsewhere (`forecasting-honesty.md`, `icp-and-qualification.md`).

This isn't a claim this pack can back with a measured outcome (§1) — it is a structural argument,
and §3 gives it the strongest exhibit found in this research.

## 3. The exit-criteria exhibit

GitLab's public stage-gate handbook — the most detailed sales-process artifact found anywhere in
this research, permalinked and CC BY-SA 4.0 prose — does not treat the demo as a freestanding
event. Its **3-Technical Evaluation** stage exit criteria include: demo output shared with the
customer, ROI measures determined and validated, mutual agreement to move to proposal with
identified next steps, and specific MEDDPPICC elements (Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Paper
Process) confirmed
([GitLab opportunity-stages handbook](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-com/content-sites/handbook/-/blob/46180fa3686c8baa55f91bf32c6e1d676eefd475/content/handbook/sales/commercial/comm-sales-opp-stages/_index.md),
PRIMARY, permalinked).

Three details worth noticing in that design: it is the demo's **output** that gets shared, not the
demo event itself; it sits gated behind ROI measures being validated, not scheduled by default the
moment a call is booked; and it exits into agreement on next steps, not into a close. A demo
without a validated ROI measure behind it has, by this design, nothing to exit into — it is a tour.

## 4. What "narrative" means here

A narrative arc for a sales call connects three things in order: what the discovery call found,
what the demo shows in response, and what objection or hesitation the sequence is likely to surface
next (→ `objections-and-enablement.md` for handling it, `proposals-pricing-and-negotiation.md` for
what follows if the demo lands). The ordering matters more than any individual technique: findings
first, then the walkthrough built to answer them, then the anticipated pushback — not a script that
opens with features and hopes the discovery call retroactively justifies them.

Several CRM pipeline templates in this research name a stage literally "Demo" or "Demo/Evaluation"
(the incumbent RevOps pipeline table surveyed in this build's census is one instance). A named
stage is a checkpoint label, not evidence the activity inside it changes an outcome — the same
distinction this pack draws for hand-set forecast stage weights generally
(`forecasting-honesty.md`). Don't let a stage existing stand in for a stage being validated; the
full stage-design and stage-naming discussion lives in `crm-and-pipeline.md`.

## 5. Practical construction

- Open with 2–3 findings from the discovery call, stated plainly, then map each to the specific
  thing the demo will show — not the reverse.
- A genuine incumbent content category exists for this: `sales-enablement/demo-scripts.md` inside
  [coreyhaines31/marketingskills](https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills) (MIT, 355
  lines, "demo scripts by call type") demonstrates that structured demo scripting is a real,
  worthwhile artifact to maintain. Cite the existence and shape of that category; do not reproduce
  its script content — the same cite-never-reproduce discipline this pack applies to every named
  methodology (the awesome-copilot MEDDICC reproduction is the cautionary exhibit for what happens
  when a pack skips this step).
- The rapport evidence covered in `discovery-calls.md` (Huang 2017, Yeomans 2019, *JPSP* —
  question-asking increases liking, studied in non-sales dyads) transfers to a demo only this far:
  asking a clarifying question mid-demo may build rapport. It is not evidence the demo itself
  changed the outcome, and a demo script may not borrow those papers' authority any more than a
  branded discovery-call methodology may.
- Close the loop: whatever the demo was built to answer becomes the thing checked against the
  actual result in win/loss review (`win-loss-learning.md`'s predict-then-score primitive) — did
  the answer the demo gave actually address what the discovery call found, or was the demo
  answering a question nobody asked.

## 6. Demo before the discovery call: the prebuilt-proof first touch

A large practitioner genre inverts §2: build the demo first and send it as the opening move. The
pipeline version states the order plainly — "by the time outreach starts, the demo is live, the
walkthrough is recorded, and the UGC video exists. all before anyone has been contacted"
([@everestchris6](https://x.com/everestchris6/status/2025291876052738163), article); the manual
version moves the pitch from "what I will do" to "see a website rebuild optimized for conversion"
([@omoalhajaabiola](https://x.com/omoalhajaabiola/status/2072228011974291667), a sponsored post); the
general advice is "If you're pitching a service, create a mock-up of what you'd do for the person and
include a screenshot in the email" ([@SahilBloom](https://x.com/SahilBloom/status/1912649564256870669)).
The argument is cost — generation made speculative work cheap enough to do per prospect. Grade: a
practitioner and course-funnel genre whose attached outcome figures are second-hand or self-reported,
method unknown. §1's null stands: nothing here measures whether demo-first wins more deals.

It is consistent with §2 under one condition: **the need must be observable from outside** — the
slow page, the missing phone number, the request the owner posted. Outside-in observation then
stands in for the discovery call's first question, and only that one; a prebuilt artifact answers
what is visible, not what the buyer prioritizes, who decides, or what else they are weighing. The
call that follows still opens with a question before the walkthrough
([discovery-calls.md](discovery-calls.md) §4's ask-for-an-instance mechanic is the one to borrow).

Three checks before a prebuilt demo goes out:
- **Built from their real details, checked before sending.** A demo carrying wrong facts about their
  business fails the observation test in [outbound-and-prospecting.md](outbound-and-prospecting.md)
  — the reader can falsify it in seconds.
- **Attach or offer — contested.** One pipeline sends the live link in the first email
  (everestchris6, above); the low-commitment school asks permission first ("Want me to send over the
  campaign structure we used for {{client 1}}?",
  [@NickAbraham12](https://x.com/NickAbraham12/status/2086451710617162198)). No source compares the
  two; links in cold mail are also a deliverability question, which is marketing's.
- **A scope promise.** Whatever the demo shows is what the buyer now expects at the quoted price; a
  gap between the two becomes the first objection (`objections-and-enablement.md`) or the first
  lost deal.

## 7. Never-ship

- Any bare demo-to-close or demo-attendance conversion percentage — none was found sourced
  anywhere in this research; treat one encountered elsewhere as folklore until a method is
  attached.
- "Demo" or "Demo/Evaluation" as a pipeline stage name, presented as if the name itself were
  evidence the stage predicts anything — see `crm-and-pipeline.md` for the stage-naming vs
  stage-validation distinction this pack draws everywhere.
- The JPSP question-asking papers (§5) cited as proof that a demo methodology, script, or framework
  improves win rates — they measure liking in conversation, not sales outcomes.
- Build-cost-versus-agency-quote posts (a few dollars of generation against a studio's five-figure
  quote) cited as evidence that demo-first wins deals (§6) — they describe the seller's cost, not
  the buyer's decision.

## Validation

- Every demo-structure claim traces to what the discovery call surfaced for that specific deal, not
  to a fixed script applied by default.
- No figure ships without a method attached; where none exists, §1's honest position is stated
  rather than implied.
- Objection anticipation and post-demo proposal sequencing are cross-linked to their owning files,
  never restated in full here.
- A prebuilt-demo first touch names the outside-observable need it answers, and the call that
  follows still opens with a question (§6).

## Failure modes and handoff

- **"What's the proven demo script?"** — none is evidenced; give §1's honest position and §5's
  construction method instead.
- **"Should I send a mockup in the cold email?"** — §6: only where the need is observable from
  outside, built from their real details; attach-versus-offer stays contested.
- **An objection surfaces during or right after the demo** — → `objections-and-enablement.md`.
- **What happens after a demo that lands (quote, proposal)** — →
  `proposals-pricing-and-negotiation.md`.
- **Whether question-asking evidence validates a demo or discovery-call methodology** — →
  `discovery-calls.md`, which carries the full rapport-vs-win-rate distinction for both.
