## VERDICT FIRST

**The named sales methodologies are not evidence-based, and the field's single largest empirical
claim dissolves on inspection.** Neil Rackham's famous SPIN study — 35,000 calls, 12 years, 20+
countries — is not published anywhere reviewable. D1 followed Wikipedia's citation chain for that
claim to its actual source and found **an archived copy of Rackham's own speaker-bureau bio page**.
Huthwaite's own blog restates the scale in Rackham's words and adds no citation, no dates, no
country count, no funding figure, and no link to any report or dataset. Of the named methodologies
examined, **exactly one — Challenger — has ever attracted a peer-reviewed critique** (Rapp,
Bachrach, Panagopoulos & Ogilvie 2014, *JPSSM* 34(4):245–259, a Jolson Award winner). MEDDIC has
attracted no academic attention at all. BANT's IBM origin is **confirmed folklore**: no primary IBM
document, no named individual, no agreed decade, and no Wikipedia article exists for it at all.
Sandler and Gap Selling make no empirical claim to evaluate — which is a different null result, and
the pack must never describe them as "research-backed," because no such claim was ever made.

**The falsification strips split three ways, and that split is the useful finding.** Unlike the
`success` build — where zero strips survived — one of ours survives with honours. **The "57%" figure
is real, traceable, and well-documented**; D2 recovered the actual 47-page CEB/Google primary and I
independently re-downloaded and re-grepped it to confirm. What is folklore is the *popular
restatement*. At the other extreme, the persistence statistics ("92% give up after four nos") are
pure folklore whose most-cited attribution is an anonymous, now-dead blog post that names no study.
In the middle sits a vendor-research genre (RAIN Group, Gong Labs, HubSpot benchmarks) with real
samples, undisclosed methods, and no public data.

**The pack is not left without evidence — it is left without evidence from the sales canon.** The
lead slice found a strong peer-reviewed layer that the canon never cites: two meta-analyses of
salesperson performance, a 26-million-observation PNAS field study of negotiation, and a 1977
*Psychological Review* paper that undercuts win/loss interviewing at its root. **This is the wedge
shape the family has found in every recent build** — the incumbent literature's central inferential
move is unvalidated, and better evidence exists one discipline over.

**On forecasting, the evidence holds but the POSTURE is prior art.** A 23.6k★ incumbent already
preaches assumption-block discipline (§5.0) — **we cannot claim novelty on "name your assumptions."**
What channel D contributes is the evidence line beneath it, plus a null that survives that
incumbent: nobody — no vendor, no
analyst, no academic — publishes a backtest calibrating CRM stage-weight percentages against actual
close outcomes, and no sales-specific calibration curve exists anywhere D3 could reach. GitLab, which
the charter guessed would supply real forecast categories, publishes its **stage** process but no
probability weights at all (verified by grepping the raw handbook markdown: zero hits). Every
forecasting vendor asserts accuracy and **none** discloses what accuracy is measured against, over
what horizon, or on whose data — and the same is true of the best incumbent skill. What *does* exist
is a strong peer-reviewed judgmental-forecasting
literature the sales field never cites — including a documented account of a multinational that
"always ensure[s] that sales are within 0.5% of the forecast — they made sure it happened that way,"
and a survey finding that **only 52.8% of professional forecasters ever check whether their own
adjustments improve accuracy.**

**One process finding the controller must act on.** My independent verification of D1's MEDDPICC
ruling — sourced by the worker from **the winning party's own press release** — found the core
holding correct but **two surrounding characterisations wrong, one of them inverted**. Details in
§6. The lesson generalises: a party-issued press release about a favourable ruling is not a primary
source for what the ruling said.

### The two findings inside strip 1 that the pack should build on

**First, CEB itself introduced the drift.** The primary's body text says "more than one-half," Figure
1 plots "57% Complete" (n=1,500), and the **sidebar pull-quote on the very same page** reads:

> "On average, customers progress **nearly 60%** of the way through the purchase decision-making
> process before engaging a sales rep."

I verified this independently: I re-downloaded the 47-page PDF (HTTP 200) and grepped it. Both the
"57% Complete" figure and the "nearly 60%" sidebar are present, verbatim, in the same document. The
rounding that everyone blames on careless bloggers was performed first by the research's own
publisher.

**Second, the 70% is a different quantity entirely.** Figure 2's "up to 70%" is the maximum of a
distribution **across the 22 supplier organisations**, not across customers. "70% of buyers" is
therefore not a stronger version of the same claim; it is a category error.

**Third, the two famous numbers are not comparable and share a lineage that makes conflation
tempting.** CEB's 57% is decision-completion; Gartner's 17% is time-allocation. They are routinely
cited back-to-back as mutual corroboration. Both trace to **Brent Adamson**, who co-authored the
2012 HBR piece at CEB and was the named Gartner VP behind the 17% after Gartner acquired CEB in 2017.

**Fourth — a structural hazard worth teaching:** the Gartner page at the live URL **no longer
contains the 17% figure**. Any current article citing that URL for that number is citing a page
whose content changed underneath it. The wording survives only in the Wayback capture.

## 4. AI outbound: the hard anchors

### Google's sender requirements — live-verified, and the number everyone gets wrong

D4 fetched `https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126` live (footer "©2026 Google") and grepped the
raw text rather than trusting a summary — the charter's hardest rule, correctly applied.

- Effective **February 1, 2024**; bulk tier is **"more than 5,000 messages per day to Gmail accounts."**
- **All senders: SPF or DKIM. Bulk senders: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC** (enforcement policy may be `p=none`).
- One-click unsubscribe required for marketing/subscribed mail; `List-Unsubscribe-Post` + `List-Unsubscribe` headers; **RFC 8058**.
- **The spam-rate nuance — two numbers with different status:**
  > "Keep spam rates reported in Postmaster Tools below **0.30%**." (the requirement)
  > "Keep spam rates reported in Postmaster Tools below **0.10%** and avoid ever reaching a spam rate of **0.30%** or higher." (the guidance)

  **0.30% is the hard line; 0.10% is the target.** Collapsing to one number loses either the
  requirement or the actionable margin.
- **Yahoo's own page states no numeric bulk threshold.** The universally repeated "5,000/day for
  Google and Yahoo" is primary for Google and **secondary-only for Yahoo**. Do not fuse them.

Per the charter, `marketing` owns deliverability doctrine and consent regimes — sales **cites** this
anchor, never restates the tutorial.

### The AI-SDR reality check

All vendor claims (Artisan, 11x, Clay, Regie.ai, AiSDR, Qualified) grade **VENDOR-SELF-REPORTED**.
The one case independently checked by an outside party did not hold up: TechCrunch, 2025-03-24,
"a16z and Benchmark backed 11x has been claiming customers it doesn't have," drawing on ~two dozen
sources including investors and current/former employees.

- **ZoomInfo**: not a customer — a one-month trial only. On record: *"We did not give them permission
  to use our logo"*, and the product *"performed significantly worse than our SDR employees."*
- **Airtable**: not a customer — *"It was never used in production and never rolled out to our sales
  team."*
- **Two contested pairs, both inside the same article:** ARR "approaching $10M" (company) vs ~$3M
  (employee source); retention 79% (company) vs "losing 70–80% of customers" (employee source).
  **Never cite either side alone.**

**Deliverability "collapse": an honest null.** No market-wide, independently measured reply-rate
trend exists in what D4 could reach. Vendor case studies report figures as high as 60–70% positive
reply rates, which are cherry-picked and must never be presented as benchmarks. The defensible
evidence is *structural*: an entire paid product category (warmup, dedicated sending infra,
placement monitoring) exists, which tells you senders are paying to fight a real problem — a
revealed-preference signal rather than a vendor statistic.

### Disclosure obligations — two jurisdictions converge

- **EU AI Act Art. 50(1)**, verified against the Official Journal text of Reg. (EU) 2024/1689:
  providers must ensure people "are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless
  this is obvious…"; Art. 50(5) requires this "at the latest at the time of the first interaction."
- **California SB 1001** (Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17940–17943), read from the statute by the lead
  because D4 correctly declined to guess. Its prohibition targets bots used *"in order to incentivize
  a purchase or sale of goods or services in a commercial transaction"* — **literally AI-SDR
  conduct** — and it is a **safe-harbour statute: disclosure cures liability.**
  **Correction to a widely repeated claim:** the "10 million monthly visitors" threshold does **not**
  gate the prohibition. § 17941(a) is triggered by communicating *"online"* (§ 17940(b), **no
  threshold**); the threshold-bearing term *"online platform"* (§ 17940(c)) appears operatively only
  in § 17942(c)'s **service-provider carve-out**. A solo founder running an AI SDR at EU or
  California recipients is within scope on the face of both texts.

This gives the pack a concrete, sourced AI-sales policy rule rather than a vibe: **disclose the
agent, clearly, at first contact** — mirroring `success`'s AI-support-policy precedent.

### 5a. THE HEADLINE NULL — nobody publishes a backtest of stage-weighted pipeline probabilities

**No published backtest or calibration study of CRM stage-weight percentages was found by any route.**

### 5d. The judgmental-forecasting literature — the best evidence base in the whole channel

Two papers, both **downloaded as open-access PDFs and read directly** with `pdftotext`:

**Lawrence, Goodwin, O'Connor & Önkal (2006), "Judgmental forecasting: A review of progress over the
last 25 years," *International Journal of Forecasting* 22(3):493–518. DOI 10.1016/j.ijforecast.2006.03.007.**
It has a dedicated §3.3 on sales forecasting, and it contains the single best teaching quote found
anywhere in this research:

> "In another case, sales levels were manipulated by promotional spending to ensure that sales targets
> were met. **One major multi-national commented that they always ensure that sales are within 0.5%
> of the forecast — they made sure it happened that way!**"

immediately followed by the review authors' own admission: *"To our knowledge, this aspect of the
sales forecasting process has been largely ignored by researchers."* And the related finding that
where a forecast doubles as a target, *"if the forecast was used as a target to be achieved, the
forecasts tended to be below the actual"* — **sandbagging is a documented, peer-reviewed phenomenon,
not folklore.** The paper also supplies a citable academic definition of **calibration**.

**Fildes & Goodwin (2007), "Against Your Better Judgment?", *Interfaces* 37(6):570–576.
DOI 10.1287/inte.1070.0309** — survey of **120 forecasters** (method disclosed: 115 recruited at four
international forecasting conferences, plus 5 from case studies). Its damning result:

> Asked whether they ever review the extent to which judgmental adjustments improve accuracy,
> **"Only 52.8% of respondents said they did"** — and **"25% did not indicate that they used any
> error measure at all."**

In one case organisation the forecasting software "produced forecasts less accurate than the random
walk, yet the result was unknown to the company forecasters." **This is the honest, method-bearing
replacement for the untraceable "forecast accuracy is under 50%" folklore.**

Also catalogued with DOIs (abstract- or title-only verified, flagged as such): Bonney, Plouffe &
Hochstein 2020 (*IMM*, rep-vs-manager opportunity overconfidence); Mantrala & Raman 1990 (*IJRM*, the
earliest formal treatment of incentive design ↔ forecast bias — the root of sandbagging); McCarthy
Byrne, Moon & Mentzer 2011 (*IMM*); Hoyle, Dingus & Wilson 2020 (*J. Marketing Analytics*); Eitle &
Buxmann 2019 (HICSS — ML on real CRM pipeline data, finding early-stage outcomes are the hardest to
predict).

### 5e. Small numbers — where the pack's distinctive teaching lives

Three-way convergent definition of calibration (Lawrence et al. 2006 academic; Wikipedia's Brier
score article; Good Judgment Open's live-tournament explainer), plus the statistical fact that
underwrites the whole argument, verbatim from the Brier score article:

> "Quite large sample sizes, i.e. **n > 1000**, are required for higher-skill forecasts of relatively
> rare events, whereas only quite modest sample sizes are needed for low-skill forecasts of common
> events."

**A solo founder's pipeline is nowhere near that.** The defensible pack rule: *a single deal's "60%"
is not falsifiable; calibration is a property of a track record across many same-type deals, not of
any one forecast.* Good Judgment Open makes the same point operationally — scores are averaged across
a question's lifespan and across many questions. Note the scale caveat D3 caught: Brier is 0–1 in the
Wikipedia formulation and 0–2 in Good Judgment's multi-option one — **do not conflate the scales.**

### 5f. "3x pipeline coverage" is a tautology

The rule is simply **coverage = 1 / win rate**. At a 25% win rate you need 4x; at 35%, roughly 3x. The
famous "3x" is just what falls out of assuming a ~30–35% win rate. No independent study establishing
3x as an empirically discovered threshold exists — every source found derives it from the same
arithmetic identity. **Teach the derivation, never the constant.**

### 5g. KBCM — a correction to this charter's own instruction

The charter (and my brief to D3) stated the KBCM quota figure was "already primary-verified" in the
`success` corpus and should be reused, not re-derived. **D3 read that corpus and found otherwise.**
The subject is correct — it is **AE (sales rep) quota attainment ~70%→75%**, explicitly *not* a CSM
figure, and the sibling build's own disposition was to never ship the "CSM quota attainment 55–65%"
version. **But the sibling corpus records no permalink for it** — it lists the materials searched
(Sapphire Ventures press page, PRNewswire, KeyBanc IR releases, a 2024 survey landing page, a
SaaSletter writeup) without capturing the URL the quote came from. Year is *likely 2024, not
confirmed*.

**Disposition: treat ~70%→75% AE quota attainment as SECONDARY with an unresolved permalink**, not as
primary-verified. **Flagged to the controller as a cross-pack citation-integrity issue** — a figure
believed verified by two builds turns out to have no recorded source.

### 6a. Win/loss learning rests on an inference psychology falsified in 1977

**Nisbett, R. E., & Wilson, T. D. (1977), *Psychological Review* 84(3):231–259** — full text read via
`curl` + `pdftotext`. PRIMARY.

> "Evidence is reviewed which suggests that there may be **little or no direct introspective access
> to higher order cognitive processes**… when people attempt to report on their cognitive processes…
> they do not do so on the basis of any true introspection. Instead, their reports are based on **a
> priori, implicit causal theories, or judgments about the extent to which a particular stimulus is a
> plausible cause of a given response.**"

The decisive study is a **consumer-choice** experiment: **four identical pairs of nylon stockings**,
n=52, in a commercial setting. The right-most pair was preferred **~4:1**; *"no subject ever
mentioned spontaneously the position"* and when asked directly *"virtually all subjects denied it."*
The items were identical, so every stated reason was necessarily a confabulation.

**The sharpest result is the observer-subject finding:** *"The observer subjects made predictions
that in every case were similar to the erroneous reports given by the actual subjects"* — and both
were *slightly negatively* correlated with the true effects. **A stated reason therefore carries no
privileged information about the actual cause.** Applied to win/loss: "we went with X because of
price" is evidence about what sounds plausible to the buyer, not about what caused the loss.

The boundary condition must be carried honestly — the authors say accurate reports *do* occur "when
influential stimuli are salient and are plausible causes." So the teaching is **not** "win/loss
interviews are worthless"; it is that stated reasons are least reliable for exactly the subtle,
competitive, relational factors win/loss most wants to learn, and the interviewer cannot tell which
case they are in from the answer alone.

**Spot check, scoped honestly:** the leading vendor's flagship guidance
(Clozd, "Win-Loss 101: What to Ask Buyers") contains **no acknowledgment of self-report limitations**
— its rationale is *consistency* ("you don't have enough consistent data to identify any meaningful
trends"), not validity. That is one page from one vendor, checked once; it supports the narrow claim
that standard practitioner guidance treats a stated reason as data rather than as testimony to be
corroborated. It does **not** support "the whole industry ignores validity" — that would need the
grep-verified uniqueness check the family's rules require, and it was not done.

### 6b. Two meta-analyses that contradict the canon's universalism

- **Franke & Park (2006), *JMR* 43(4):693–702** — 155 samples, 31,000+ salespeople. *"ASB increases
  self-rated, manager-rated, and objective measures of performance, whereas **CO increases only
  self-rated performance**."* Correlations span **−.16 to .35**. Two teachings: the **self-rating
  trap** (customer orientation looks effective only in the measure most vulnerable to self-image),
  and a **calibration against transformation-grade claims** — nothing credible in this literature
  supports them.
- **Kerr & Marcos-Cuevas (2023), *JPSSM*** — 150 studies, 936 raw effects, 2009–2020. Determinants
  classify along a continuum *"ranging from universal, to context-specific to necessary but
  insufficient"*, and *"the operationalization of salesperson performance"* significantly changes
  which determinants appear to matter. **This is the academic warrant for the pack refusing to
  prescribe one methodology.**

### 6c. "Always anchor first" is CONTESTED, with permalinks on both sides

- **Schweinsberg et al. (2023), *PNAS*** — **26 million eBay negotiations**: a *"linear anchoring
  effect on price and **nonlinear effects on impasse risk**"*, explicitly *"incompatible with current
  theorizing."* The shape the canon never teaches: price gains rise roughly linearly with ambition
  while the risk of no deal rises non-linearly. (Consumer marketplace, buyer-side offers — the shape
  transfers, the numbers do not.)
- **Maaravi & Levy (2017), *Judgment and Decision Making*** — under **information asymmetry**, *"it
  is actually preferable for both counterparts not to give the first offer."* Directly relevant to
  pricing presentation, which the charter assigns to sales.

Per charter R21(c), a claim with both sides permalinked is worth carrying — so this belongs in the
pack as a **contested doctrine with boundary conditions**, not as a rule.

### 6d. Discovery questioning — real evidence, but not for what the canon claims

Recovered on re-audit (these were a tooling null on first pass — see the null-result audit):

- **Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), "It doesn't hurt to ask: Question-asking increases
  liking," *JPSP*.** DOI 10.1037/pspi0000097
- **Yeomans, Brooks, Huang, Minson & Gino (2019), "It helps to ask: The cumulative benefits of asking
  follow-up questions," *JPSP*.** DOI 10.1037/pspi0000220

Both PRIMARY. **The caveat is load-bearing:** these study question-asking in *conversation* and its
effect on *liking* (speed-dating and get-to-know-you dyads), **not sales discovery calls**. They
establish that asking questions — especially follow-ups — makes a counterpart like you more. They do
**not** establish that any sales questioning framework improves deal outcomes, and must never be used
to retroactively validate SPIN, whose own study is unpublished (§1).

**The precise, defensible position: good evidence that question-asking builds rapport; no published
evidence that any branded sales questioning methodology improves win rates.** The canon conflates
these routinely, and the gap between them is teachable.

## Candidate never-ship figures (channel D consolidated)

**Never see it** — no defensible version exists:
1. "92% of salespeople give up after 4 nos / 80% of prospects say no 4 times." Folklore; source is an anonymous dead blog naming no study.
2. "80% of sales require 5 follow-up calls." Same; and its breakdown contradicts the other folklore version.
3. "17% higher sales volume" for SPIN-trained reps; "18%" / "20–30% higher win rates" for MEDDIC. Vendor marketing, zero citation.
4. Smartlead's "60–70% positive reply rates." One cherry-picked case study, not a benchmark.
5. "Over 70% of enterprise buyers identify a vendor before a budget exists." Uncredited vendor statistic.
5a. **"CSM quota attainment 55–65%" attributed to KBCM.** Confirmed misattribution — the real figure is AE quota attainment. Never ship the CSM version under any circumstances.
5b. **"CSO Insights: forecast accuracy is ~50%" / "under half of forecasted deals close."** Untraceable to any primary. Use Fildes & Goodwin (2007) instead — peer-reviewed, method-disclosed, makes the same point honestly.
5c. **Any bare vendor forecast-accuracy percentage** (Gong's "95%", "20% more precision", Aviso's "29 quarters"). None disclose method. Never ship without the vendor's own method statement attached, and even then label it vendor-self-reported.
5d. **A "Salesforce forecast category" named "Omitted."** Unverified; HubSpot's verified equivalent is "Not forecasted." Do not assume the taxonomies match.
5e. **RepVue-sourced numbers of any kind.** The site was Cloudflare-blocked to every fetch method; do not cite RepVue figures from memory.

**Recognise-and-correct** — in wide enough circulation that the correction is the teaching:
6. **"57% of the buying journey is over before you talk to sales."** Teach the real figure (n=1,500, self-reported, "before engaging a supplier sales *rep*"), that **CEB's own sidebar** rounded it to "nearly 60%", and that the "70%" is a **supplier-level maximum**, not a buyer figure.
7. **Gartner's 17% cited as confirming the 57%.** Different quantities (time vs. completion); same institutional lineage; and the live URL no longer contains the figure.
8. **"8 touches."** A real named study exists (RAIN Group, 489 sellers) but "touchpoint" is undefined and the genre ranges 5–15 with most citing nothing.
9. **Gong-Labs-style "N million calls → Y% higher win rate."** Teach how to read it: real N, undisclosed construction, customer-base selection bias, correlation without confound control, no public data.
10. **"Industry average win rate is 21%."** Teach the denominator problem, not a ban.
11. **"Always Be Closing."** One sentence: its famous instance is a fictional antagonist in a 1992 film satirising the mentality the phrase is used to celebrate — and the character is not in Mamet's play.
10a. **Question-asking evidence cited as validation of a sales questioning methodology.** The two *JPSP* papers measure *liking* in conversation, not win rates. Teach the distinction; never let SPIN borrow their authority.
11a. **"3x pipeline coverage."** Never as a portable constant — it is 1/win-rate restated. Ship the derivation with the reader's own win rate, or not at all.
11b. **"~70%→75% AE quota attainment" (KBCM).** Subject is correct but **no permalink exists**, in this corpus or the sibling's. SECONDARY at best; do not present as primary-verified.
12. **Gmail's spam threshold as a single number.** Always both: 0.30% requirement, 0.10% target.
13. **"Google and Yahoo both use 5,000/day."** Primary for Google only; secondary for Yahoo.
14. **11x's ARR/retention.** Never one side without the other, and always name the source type.
15. **"California's bot law only applies to platforms with 10M+ visitors."** A never-ship claim in *inverted* form — the danger is a reader concluding they are exempt. Falsified by the statute's own structure.
16. **PTC's revenue growth attributed to MEDDIC.** Two conflicting versions now exist — the vendor's "$300M→$1B in four years" and the **court opinion's** "$1 million in 1989 to $1.1 billion in 1998." The opinion's version will be more quotable *because* it is in a federal opinion; the court was reciting background, **not making a causal finding**. Ship neither as evidence the methodology works.

**Ship, but only with their subject attached** (charter's verify-the-subject rule):
17. The stockings **4:1** ratio — n=52, four **identical** pairs. Never "consumers prefer right-hand options 4:1."
18. Franke & Park's **31,000 salespeople** — 155 samples pooled in a meta-analysis, not one study or firm.
19. Schweinsberg's **26 million negotiations** — eBay Best Offer, consumer marketplace, buyer-side. Not B2B evidence.
20. Franke & Park's **−.16 to .35** range — worth shipping *as* the teaching (R21 clause b), to calibrate against vendor claims.

### CORRECTION — the discovery-questioning gap is now CLOSED (with a caveat that must travel)

The peer-reviewed literature does exist: **question-asking, and especially follow-up questions,
increases how much a counterpart likes you** (two *JPSP* papers, above). **But these are studies of
conversation and liking — speed-dating and get-to-know-you dyads — not of sales discovery calls.**
They must never be used to retroactively validate SPIN.

The honest position is therefore sharper than the original null: **there is good evidence that
question-asking builds rapport, and no published evidence that any branded sales questioning
methodology improves win rates.** Those are different claims, and the canon routinely conflates
them. The gap between them is itself teachable.
